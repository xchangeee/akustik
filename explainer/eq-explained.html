1 · The problem

Your room is a resonator

Between two parallel walls, a sound wave whose half wavelength fits the gap exactly bounces back and forth and builds itself up. That's a standing wave, or room mode. Its frequency is f = n · 343 / (2 · distance).

A mode has fixed loud spots (always at the walls) and quiet spots (nulls) in between. Drag the frequency below and watch where the pressure piles up. The lower chart shows how loud each spot gets at every frequency.

Across the 3.9 m width: speaker wall → sofa

Two things follow for this room:

  • The sofa is against a wall, so every width mode (44, 88 Hz) is at its loudest exactly where you sit.
  • L sits in the middle of the 6 m wall, which is the null of the odd length modes (29, 86 Hz). R sits near the side wall and drives all of them. The two speakers are identical, yet R measured with much more bass. Switch to "Length 6.0 m" to see it.
2 · The rule

Peaks can be cut, dips can't be filled

A dip is usually two waves arriving out of step (the direct sound and a reflection, or the left and the right speaker) and cancelling each other. Turning that frequency up makes both waves bigger, and they still cancel. A peak is the opposite: waves adding up, and turning down really lowers it. Try it.

So the design is cut only. Cutting the room's peaks evens out the response at the sofa and reduces the energy that rings through the room and leaks into the staircase. Boosting dips would only blast the rest of the room.

3 · Measuring

A sweep becomes an impulse response

REW plays a tone gliding from 10 Hz to 22 kHz in 11 seconds and records it. Dividing the recording by the original sweep gives the impulse response: what the mic would hear if the speaker played one infinitely short click. Because speaker and room behave linearly, this one curve describes everything about the path from speaker to mic.

One mic position is a snapshot; move your head 30 cm and the curve changes. That's why each speaker was measured at 7 spots around the sofa. The spread between them is the most useful clue in the whole project: a room mode stays put when the mic moves; a cancellation dip jumps around.

4 · Both speakers together

Left plus right is not left and right

Music has mostly mono bass: both speakers play the same bass at once. At your ear the two waves add with their timing, so they can reinforce or cancel. Thanks to the loopback timing reference, every measurement has the same time zero, so adding the left and right measurements gives exactly what the mic would have heard with both playing.

5 · The tool

One EQ band, three knobs

Each band is a peaking filter: a bell-shaped cut around a centre frequency. It has three settings: frequency (where), gain (how deep) and Q (how narrow; the width is about frequency ÷ Q). Inside the DSP each band is a tiny formula, a biquad, that runs 48 000 times a second.

Preset: band 1 of the left channel. A room mode is about as wide as 2.2 ÷ decay time: with 0.5 s of ringing that's ~4.4 Hz, a Q of about 10 at 43 Hz. That's why the bands here sit at Q 5–8.

6 · The central trick

Simulate first, measure later

Speaker, room and filter are all linear, so their effects simply multiply, frequency by frequency. Multiply each of the 32 recorded measurements by a filter's response and you get exactly what the mic would have recorded with that EQ on, without anyone re-measuring. This chart does that live in your browser. Switch bands on and off and watch the result.

7 · Letting the computer choose

What counts as "better"

Finding 7 bands per channel by hand is guesswork, so a numerical optimiser did it. It needs a single score for "how bad is this EQ". The score here is asymmetric:

  • bass above the target (red) counts in full: those are the peaks to remove;
  • bass below the target (blue) counts only if the EQ made it deeper. An existing dip costs nothing, because EQ can't fix it anyway.

The score adds up four curves: the sofa with both speakers (weight 1.0), the sofa with each speaker alone (0.5 each), and the DJ spot with both (0.4). Each dB of cut costs a tiny bit extra, so no band is spent on nothing.

8 · Speaking the DSP's language

Fitting the design onto the device

The DSP only takes values on fixed steps (from your reverse-engineered protocol): gain in 0.1 dB, frequency in 1/30-octave steps, Q in 1/12-octave steps. The design was rounded onto those steps, then nudged one step at a time wherever that improved the score. Rounding cost practically nothing (score 11.57 → 11.61).

One more twist: the device's Q is not the textbook Q. Its peaking filter is the standard one, but with Qtextbook = Qdevice ÷ A, where A depends on the gain. Move the gain:

Ruler: the DSP's frequency steps between 35 and 140 Hz. The dots are your 12 bands; each sits exactly on a step.

The filter files use REW's text format only because your configurator imports that format. Its import converts the file's textbook Q into device Q and rounds to the steps, so the script wrote values that land exactly where the design wants them. Running the files through the configurator's own import code confirmed it: no errors, identical values.

9 · Proof

Does the DSP do what the model says?

The simulation is only as good as its assumption that the DSP builds exactly these filters. So the DSP outputs were wired straight into the audio interface (no amp, no speaker, no mic) and measured.

DSP output, measured electrically

From 30 to 150 Hz the two lines differ by a few hundredths of a dB. Below 30 Hz the measured line drops up to 1 dB further: that's the converters of the interface and DSP, not the EQ.

10 · Limits

What EQ can't do, and what's next

  • Dips stay. R's dip at 173 Hz (reflection off the wall behind it), the DJ spot's hole at 55–75 Hz (L and R cancel there) and its weak 30–35 Hz. These need speaker moves, subwoofers or absorbers.
  • Ringing slows down only with absorption. The EQ feeds the modes less energy, but the room still decays at its own pace.
  • It's designed for the sofa and the DJ spot. Elsewhere it's better than before (less energy everywhere) but not flat.
