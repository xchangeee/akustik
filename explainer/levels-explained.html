A measurement is a loop: it starts as numbers (dBFS), becomes voltage (dBu, V), becomes air pressure (dB SPL), becomes voltage again at the mic and ends as numbers again. Each arrow is a converter, and each converter has one calibration number that links the ruler before it to the ruler after it. That's the whole secret, and section 3 lets you play with it.

1 · The bare dB

A decibel is a ratio, nothing more

A plain "dB" never means a loudness. It compares two things: dB = 20 · log₁₀(a / b) for amplitudes (voltage, air pressure, sample values) and 10 · log₁₀(P₁ / P₂) for power. Both give the same number for the same change, because power grows with amplitude squared.

Where the rules of thumb come from. log₁₀(2) = 0.301, so twice the power is 10 · 0.301 = 3 dB. Power goes with amplitude squared (P = V²/R, and the same for pressure), and a logarithm turns a square into a factor of 2: 10 · log₁₀(V₁²/V₂²) = 20 · log₁₀(V₁/V₂). So twice the voltage is 20 · 0.301 = 6 dB, which is the same as 4× the power (10 · log₁₀ 4 = 6). Two sounds add wave by wave, and the power goes with the square of the sum: (p₁ + p₂)² = p₁² + p₂² + 2·p₁·p₂. For two unrelated sounds the cross term 2·p₁·p₂ averages to zero over time, so the powers add: two equal ones give twice the power, +3 dB. For the same signal in step, p₁ = p₂, the pressure doubles: +6 dB. Exactly opposite, p₂ = −p₁: silence (section 5).

Gain knobs, faders, EQ bands and "R is 2 dB louder than L" are all plain dB: they say how much more or less, not how much. 0 dB means "unchanged", not "off".

Why bother with logarithms? Because the ear works that way. Going from 1 to 2 watts sounds like the same step as going from 50 to 100 watts: both are +3 dB. And it keeps the numbers small: the quietest sound you can hear and the loudest you can bear differ by a factor of about 3 million in pressure, which is just 130 dB.

Two more things fall out: dB add up where ratios multiply. A +29 dB amplifier after a −10 dB volume knob is +19 dB in total. And about 1 dB is the smallest change most people notice in music; 3 dB is clear; 10 dB sounds roughly "twice as loud".

2 · The suffix

Add a reference, get an absolute level

As soon as the second thing in the ratio is a fixed, agreed reference, a dB value becomes an absolute level, and the suffix tells you which reference. The formula never changes, only the "1" it is measured against:

dBu and dBV are the same ruler shifted by 2.2 dB (dBu = dBV + 2.2). "Pro" line level (+4 dBu, 1.23 V) is about 12 dB hotter than "consumer" line level (−10 dBV, 0.32 V). That's why a cheap source into a pro DSP looks quiet.

3 · Putting it together

Follow one signal through the chain

Every box has a ceiling (it clips: the waveform gets flattened, which sounds harsh and can damage tweeters) and a floor (its own hiss and hum, the noise floor). The distance from the signal up to the ceiling is headroom; the distance down to the floor is the signal-to-noise ratio. Setting every box so the signal sits comfortably between both is called gain staging.

What to take away:

  • Turn down late, not early. Every box adds its own noise at a fixed level. If you shrink the signal at the start (output knob) and make it up later (DSP gain, amp), the early noise gets amplified with it. Run the signal fairly hot through the line-level gear and set loudness at the amp.
  • Peaks set the ceiling, the average sets the loudness. Music peaks 10–20 dB above its average, so an amp that shows "a few watts" on average still needs tens of watts for the drum hits.
  • dBFS is only meaningful inside one device. −20 dBFS in REW and −20 dBFS inside the DSP are different voltages, because their converters have different calibrations.
  • Output-side changes don't break the SPL calibration; input-side changes do. Turning the amp up makes the room louder and REW correctly reads more. Touching the mic preamp gain changes the dBFS↔SPL link, so REW reads wrong until you recalibrate. Try it: change the preamp gain and watch the REW card.
4 · dB(A), dB(C), dB(Z)

Sound level meters wear filters

An SPL meter adds up the energy of all frequencies into one number. But your ear is much less sensitive to deep bass and very high treble than to the mids, especially at low volume. So meters usually measure through a weighting filter first; the letter in brackets says which one. The level is still dB re 20 µPa, just of a filtered signal.

The three weighting curves

A models the ear at quiet levels: −30 dB at 50 Hz, so bass barely counts. C models the ear at loud levels: flat down to about 50 Hz, −3 dB at 31.5 Hz. Z ("zero") is no filter at all, the plain dB SPL. Hover for values.

Why two curves: the ear's bass sensitivity depends on level

Play a 1 kHz tone, then ask listeners to set a 50 Hz tone so it sounds equally loud. The 50 Hz tone always needs more SPL, but how much more depends on how loud the reference is. These matches are the equal-loudness contours (ISO 226); one contour per loudness level, measured in phon (n phon = as loud as n dB SPL at 1 kHz).

The bass penalty shrinks as the level rises: at 50 Hz from +38 dB (quiet) to +18 dB (loud). Put the other way, bass loudness grows faster than midrange loudness. At 50 Hz, going from 40 to 90 phon takes only about 30 dB more SPL, so every 10 dB turned up adds about 17 phon of bass loudness and 10 phon in the mids. That's why music sounds thin when it's quiet and much bassier when it's loud, and why old hi-fi amps had a "loudness" button.

No single filter can follow a moving target, so the standard defines several. A was derived from a quiet (40 phon) contour and C from a very loud (100 phon) one. Both are simplified fits to the 1930s contour data, so neither matches the modern table exactly. A's −30 dB at 50 Hz sits near today's 60 phon contour. C is flatter than even the 90 phon row, so it's better described as "nearly unweighted" than as an accurate model of loud listening.

The same effect shows up in EQ target curves, but don't mix the two ideas up. A preference target like the Harman in-room curve (a bass rise of several dB below ~100 Hz) comes from listening tests at a normal listening level. It's what people liked, not a correction for one loudness level, and listeners still preferred some bass rise at higher levels. Level-dependent correction is a separate feature, loudness compensation (Audyssey Dynamic EQ, Dirac Live's loudness option, the old loudness button). It adds bass as the volume goes down, by roughly the difference between the contour rows above. In practice the two combine: a quiet room benefits from more bass rise, and at loud levels less bass rise sounds balanced, and less bass rise also means less leaking out of the room and more headroom.

For bass work this matters a lot:

  • dB(A) hides bass. Doubling the bass of a song can move dB(A) by less than a decibel. A neighbour annoyed by thumping bass can be "within the dB(A) limit".
  • A big gap between dB(C) and dB(A) means "lots of low end". Pink noise has a gap of a dB or two; bass-heavy music 5–10 dB; a pure sub tone 30+.
  • For calibrating a measuring setup with pink noise and for loud listening, use C (or Z). For hearing-damage guidelines (the "85 dB" rule) the numbers are in dB(A).
5 · Adding levels

80 dB plus 80 dB is not 160 dB

Because dB are logarithms, you can't add them like plain numbers. How two sounds combine depends on whether they are related. Two unrelated sounds (two people talking, two fans, two noise generators) add their power: +3 dB for two equal ones. Two copies of the same signal (both speakers playing mono bass) add their pressure wave by wave: anything from +6 dB (in step) to silence (exactly opposite).

Two sources at one spot

Two handy consequences: a source 10 dB quieter than another adds only 0.4 dB, so background noise 10 dB below your measurement signal hardly matters. And a single number you read for "both speakers" depends on where you stand, because the timing offset changes with position. That's exactly why left + right bass measurements look so different from each speaker alone.

6 · Peak vs average

Peaks clip, averages are heard

A level meter can measure the peak (the single largest sample) or the RMS (a kind of average energy). The gap between them is the crest factor. Digital clipping happens at the peaks, loudness and amplifier heat come from the average.

A signal in a digital system

Two RMS conventions exist for dBFS. Mathematically a full-scale sine has an RMS of −3 dBFS. Many meters (the AES-17 convention) add 3 dB so that a full-scale sine reads 0 dBFS RMS. When a generator level or a meter says "dBFS RMS", check which one it uses; it's a 3 dB difference.
